📚 Theoretical Foundation

This analysis is grounded in established economic literature on cartel detection, price rigidity, and institutional economics.

Tier 1

Price Rigidity & Business Cycles

Core theories explaining why "Sticky Prices" emerge during economic crises

1

Rotemberg, J. J., & Saloner, G. (1986)

"A Supergame-Theoretic Model of Price Wars during Booms"

American Economic Review

Relevance: The primary counter-theory we test. They argue price wars happen in booms, implying cartels are most stable during recessions—explaining the "sticky prices" during the Greek crisis.

2

Green, E. J., & Porter, R. H. (1984)

"Noncooperative Collusion under Imperfect Price Information"

Econometrica

Relevance: Foundational theory that "Price Wars" are discipline mechanisms within cartels. Periods of stability followed by sharp drops indicate "equilibrium punishment phases."

3

Stephan, A. (2012)

"Price Fixing in Crisis: Implications of an Economic Downturn for Cartels and Enforcement"

World Competition

Relevance: Directly analyzes how economic downturns affect cartel stability, arguing crises force "tacit" colluders to form "hard-core" cartels—supporting our "Crisis Cartels" hypothesis.

Tier 2

Detection Theory: Bid Rigging & Procurement

Theoretical justification for Single-Bid, Concentration, and Variance screens

4

Porter, R. H., & Zona, J. D. (1993)

"Detection of Bid Rigging in Procurement Auctions"

Journal of Political Economy

Relevance: The seminal "How-To" manual for finding cartels in public contracts. Justifies detecting "complementary bids" (fake high bids) and distinguishing them from honest high costs.

5

Abrantes-Metz, R. M., et al. (2006)

"A Variance Screen for Collusion"

International Journal of Industrial Organization

Relevance: Proves that low variance (artificial stability) predicts collusion better than high prices—the theoretical basis for detecting "flat" price lines.

6

Harrington, J. E. (2008)

"Detecting Cartels"

Handbook of Antitrust Economics

Relevance: Categorizes our screens (Behavioral vs. Structural) and explains why structural screens like Benford's Law work: cartels leave statistical footprints from coordination.

7

Bajari, P., & Ye, L. (2003)

"Deciding Between Competition and Collusion"

Review of Economics and Statistics

Relevance: Competitive bids correlate with cost variables; collusive bids correlate with each other. Supports our Network/Correlation analysis approach.

Tier 3

Institutional Economics & Development

Bridging cartel analysis to broader institutional and development theory

8

Acemoglu, D., & Robinson, J. (2006)

"Economic Backwardness in Political Perspective"

American Political Science Review

Relevance: Explains "Blocking Technologies"—Greek cartels as political elites blocking efficient competitors to preserve rents, causing the "development trap."

9

Connor, J. M. (2014)

"Cartel Detection and Screening"

Oxford Handbook of International Antitrust Economics

Relevance: Global context from the world's leading cartel archivist. Validates that our screens are recognized tools in development economics.

10

Ishii, J. (2009)

"Collusion in the US Procurement Market"

Working Paper

Relevance: Explains "Bid Rotation"—when Company A and B take turns winning, this model explains how firms allocate market share without explicit communication.
